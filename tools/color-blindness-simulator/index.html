Free Color Blindness Palette Simulator – Deuteranopia, Protanopia & Tritanopia

Over 300 million people worldwide (~1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women) experience some form of Color Vision Deficiency (CVD). Our free Color Blindness Simulator lets UI/UX designers, data visualization creators, and illustrators test 5 HEX colors side-by-side in linear light across Deuteranopia, Protanopia, Tritanopia, and Achromatopsia (Artist Tonal Value)—automatically alerting you whenever two swatches collapse into indistinguishable colors.

How Painters and Designers Build Colorblind-Safe Palettes

The single most reliable secret shared by classical painters and accessibility engineers is tonal value separation. Whenever two colors have different perceived lightness (check the bottom Achromatopsia / Value row above), they remain distinguishable under every form of color blindness. For categorical charts and UI states, pair warm Vermilion-Orange (#D55E00) with cool Cerulean/Sky Blue (#0072B2) instead of equal-value Red and Green.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common type of color blindness?

Red-green color vision deficiencies—specifically Deuteranomaly and Deuteranopia—account for over 75% of all cases. Use the Deficiency Severity slider above to test both partial anomalous trichromacy (50%–75%) and full dichromacy (100%).

How do you fix two colors that look identical to colorblind users?

Click one of the conflicting swatches in the top row and drag its Brightness slider up or down to create tonal contrast, or shift red/green hues toward orange/blue. Watch the diagnostic bar turn to ALL CLEAR once every pair exceeds perceptual threshold.

Why does Protanopia make bright red colors look dark?

Without long-wavelength L-cones, red wavelengths contribute almost no brightness signal. Pure red buttons or error text on dark backgrounds lose luminance contrast and can vanish completely for Protanopic users.
